Love as Magic: A Medievalism Reading of the Harry Potter Series in Light of New Study on Iberian Love Magic

History and Literature Love Magic July 29, 2021 · Guest post by Veronica Menaldi · Edited by Samuel Gillis Hogan

Veronica Menaldi is an Assistant Professor of Spanish at the University of Mississippi. This post draws on her research into premodern Iberian literature and its resonances in modern popular culture.

When J.K. Rowling's Harry Potter series is considered alongside the scholarship on medieval Iberian love magic, unexpected correspondences emerge — correspondences that illuminate how deeply the logic of premodern magical thinking has permeated modern fantasy literature.

In Rowling's world, love functions as the ultimate magical force, the one power that Voldemort — for all his dark arts — cannot comprehend or replicate. The distinction between "good" and "bad" love determines Harry's success and Voldemort's failure. Harry carries the protection of his mother Lily's self-sacrificial love, a force that literally deflects the Killing Curse. Voldemort, conceived through a love potion and never loved during his formative years, cannot manufacture the genuine affect that underpins the deepest magic.

This maps with striking precision onto a central paradox of medieval Iberian love magic: the practitioners employed intermediaries, required personal objects belonging to the intended victim, and produced effects of indefinite duration — but the tradition was unanimous that genuine love could not be magically manufactured or sustained. The potion or charm might compel proximity, obsession, or desire, but not the freely given bond that constituted amor in the fullest sense.

The parallel is made almost explicit by Professor Slughorn in Harry Potter and the Half-Blood Prince: "It is impossible to manufacture or imitate love." The statement could appear without modification in a medieval scholastic discussion of the limits of magia amatoria.

The Celestina Connection

A further link suggests itself through the character of Celestina Warbeck, a famous fictional wizard singer in Rowling's universe. The name may gesture to Fernando de Rojas's Celestina (1499), one of the foundational texts of Iberian literary culture, in which the character Celestina is a professional go-between who facilitates romantic (and magical) encounters for a fee. Rojas's Celestina employs love magic and operates at the intersection of desire, commerce, and sorcery — a figure whose ambiguity and power resonate strikingly with the morally complex magical practitioners of Rowling's world.

The resonance is unlikely to be accidental. Rowling's choice of "Celestina Warbeck" as the name for a beloved musical celebrity — a name that evokes both celestial powers and the most famous literary go-between in Spanish letters — suggests at minimum a cultural awareness of the tradition, if not direct citation of it.

Love Magic: Medieval Iberia and the Modern Imagination

Medieval Iberian love magic operated through an elaborate logic of substitution and contact. The practitioner required some object belonging to the beloved — a hair, a garment, a footprint in wax — to establish a sympathetic link. Intermediaries (often older women, the alcahueta or go-between figure) carried the materials and the spell to the intended target. The effects were meant to be powerful but remained, in the moral theology of the period, a violation of the beloved's free will — which is precisely why the tradition insisted that such magic produced something less than true love.

Harry Potter inherits this logic in curious ways. The love potion Amortentia, explicitly described as producing obsession rather than love, confirms the distinction. The protective magic of maternal sacrifice, by contrast, operates on an entirely different register — one that the novels treat as deeper, older, and more real than anything achievable by ordinary spellcraft.

What the comparison reveals is that popular fantasy has not abandoned the medieval logic of love magic; it has internalized and reproduced it so thoroughly that the underlying framework goes largely unmarked. Rowling's readers absorb a set of assumptions about the nature of love, magic, and their relationship that would have been legible — perhaps even conventional — to a fifteenth-century Iberian reader of Fernando de Rojas.
